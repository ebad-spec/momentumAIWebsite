Introducing MindTrace: a cognitive profile model that learns how you think, built from implicit student feedback signals

Momentum Research Team  ·  May 2026

Most study tools treat every student the same. You get a flashcard wrong, the app schedules it sooner. You get it right, the app backs off. That is the entirety of the personalization loop in most systems available today, and it has remained largely unchanged for decades.

MindTrace is our attempt to move past that ceiling. Rather than asking students to rate their own confidence or waiting for a binary right/wrong signal, MindTrace builds a cognitive profile from the behavioral residue of each session: response latency, hesitation patterns, session timing, error clustering, and the sequential structure of what trips a student up. None of this requires the student to do anything differently. The signal is already there. MindTrace reads it.

This post describes the theoretical foundation behind MindTrace, what distinguishes it from existing approaches, and what the data suggests about where this method is headed.

The problem with explicit feedback

The dominant paradigm in adaptive learning systems today relies on explicit feedback. The student is asked to self-report confidence, select a difficulty rating, or is scored on a discrete right/wrong basis. These signals are then fed into a scheduling algorithm, most commonly a variant of SM-2 or its successors, which adjusts review intervals accordingly.

The limitation of this approach is not the algorithm. It is the signal.

Explicit feedback is noisy by design. Students systematically overestimate their own recall during review, a well-documented phenomenon sometimes referred to as the fluency illusion. A student who has seen a concept three times in a session will often rate their confidence high, even when their delayed recall is poor. The feedback being collected does not reflect actual memory encoding. It reflects momentary familiarity.

Implicit signals behave differently. Response latency correlates more reliably with long-term retention than self-reported confidence does. The pattern of which concepts cluster together in a student's error profile reveals interference effects that a simple difficulty rating cannot capture. Session drop-off timing tells you something about cognitive load that no explicit input ever will.

How MindTrace works

MindTrace operates as a background inference layer. During each study session, it observes the following signals without any input from the student:

Response latency. How long a student pauses before answering a card. Latency distributions vary meaningfully between concepts a student genuinely knows versus concepts they are reconstructing in real time.

Error clustering. Which concepts fail together. Correlated failures often point to a shared underlying gap rather than isolated weaknesses, and scheduling them independently misses the relationship entirely.

Session cadence. What time of day sessions occur, how long they run, and where within a session accuracy degrades. Cognitive performance follows real temporal patterns that most apps ignore entirely.

Sequential hesitation. A student who answers card A correctly but then hesitates on card B two cards later may be exhibiting interference from A. Standard algorithms treat these as independent events.

These signals are aggregated across sessions to build a vector representation of how a given student encodes, retains, and retrieves information. That vector is the cognitive profile. It does not tell you what a student knows. It tells you how they know things, and more usefully, how they forget them.

The Day 1 to Day 28 arc

The most visible representation of how MindTrace develops over time is what we call the three-phase arc.

On Day 1, MindTrace has observed a single session. The profile is sparse. Scheduling decisions at this stage are not meaningfully different from a generic baseline.

By Day 14, the profile has accumulated enough signal to begin distinguishing this student's specific interference patterns, their reliable strengths, and the conditions under which their accuracy degrades.

By Day 28, the cognitive profile has reached a level of resolution that allows MindTrace to make scheduling decisions no generic model could replicate, because they depend on behavioral patterns that are specific to this student and invisible to any system that does not track them.

Accelerating the timeline

Twenty-eight days is a meaningful milestone, not a ceiling.

The reason traditional systems require extended observation windows is that they work with sparse, low-resolution signals. Right/wrong on each card, repeated across sessions, takes time to accumulate statistical significance at the individual concept level.

Because MindTrace reads higher-bandwidth implicit signals from every session, the profile accumulates faster. Early evidence from our internal modeling suggests that MindTrace reaches profile maturity comparable to what traditional systems achieve at Day 28 in approximately nine days under typical usage patterns.

The system is most useful precisely when students need it most, during compressed high-stakes preparation periods rather than long-horizon review.

Where MindTrace sits relative to existing tools

The current landscape of student-facing study tools can be broadly characterized along two dimensions: how deeply personalized the experience is, and how much of that personalization is driven by implicit behavioral data versus explicit user input.

Anki provides powerful scheduling with essentially no implicit personalization. Quizlet offers a polished experience but treats all users identically at the algorithmic level. Duolingo incorporates some adaptive elements but operates primarily on explicit performance data and is scoped to language learning only. General-purpose AI tools are not designed for learning retention at all.

What this means for students

The practical effect of a well-resolved cognitive profile is not just better scheduling. It is a study experience that gets more efficient the longer you use it, adapts to your actual cognitive patterns rather than a population average, and surfaces the right material at the right time without requiring you to manage the system at all.

Most students do not need more study time. They need better signal about how they are actually retaining what they are studying. MindTrace is built on the premise that the signal is already there in every session, and that reading it carefully is enough to change the outcome.

What comes next

The immediate development priorities for MindTrace are threefold.

First, reducing the cold-start window further. Nine days is a meaningful improvement over twenty-eight, but we believe profile resolution can be accelerated more aggressively with better initial session design and richer signal capture from early interactions.

Second, extending the profile beyond flashcard performance. Response patterns in longer-form study tasks, practice problems, and reading sessions carry implicit signals that MindTrace does not yet incorporate. These are the next data sources we are working toward.

Third, making the profile legible to the student. Right now MindTrace operates entirely in the background. We are exploring whether surfacing elements of the cognitive profile to students, in a form they can act on, improves outcomes further or introduces noise.
